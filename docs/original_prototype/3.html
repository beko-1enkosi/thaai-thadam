The Challenge

Why Women in Trichy Need This

Indian city infrastructure is designed for a "neutral male user." Women face a hidden "pink tax" on mobility — unsafe transitions, unpredictable costs, and trip-chaining burdens that limit their independence. Women and caregivers travel differently: shorter trips, more frequent, outside peak hours, often with strollers, groceries, or children — yet infrastructure ignores these realities. 84% of women's work trips depend on public transport, and women spend 48% of travel costs on just 18% of distance — the critical first and last mile gaps that create severe time poverty. The UNFCCC's "Avoid–Shift–Improve" framework confirms that "Shift" measures — improving public transport, walking, and shared mobility — offer the best opportunities for both women's accessibility and climate goals. Field research from Bhopal, Gwalior, and Jodhpur — three tier-2 Indian cities comparable to Trichy — confirms that 95% of women identify using public transport as unsafe, ranking it the highest unsafe place across all public spaces, surpassing even marketplaces (89%) and waiting areas (84%).

Accessibility · Goal 11

Last-mile connectivity gaps, crowded buses, expensive auto or taxi options, bus stops unequipped for sunny weather.

Sensitivity · Goal 5

Mobility barriers for women with children, inadequate pedestrian infrastructure, reduced social participation and independent travel.

Safe Mobility · Goal 16

Concerns about night safety, street harassment, poor visibility and road conditions.

Unsafe First & Last Mile

Poorly lit streets and isolated routes make walking from bus stops dangerous. Women spend 40% of travel time and 48% of costs on just 18% of distance — the critical connectivity gaps.

Inadequate Bus Stops

No seating, no shade, no lighting, and no real-time information. 84% of women's work trips depend on public transport, yet stations lack basic safety infrastructure.

Unverified Transport

No way to know if an auto-rickshaw driver is trustworthy before getting in. Auto drivers frequently refuse meter fares, charging arbitrary prices based on perceived urgency — a hidden "pink tax."

No Emergency Support

When something goes wrong, women lack quick access to help. Safety infrastructure that works during the day becomes inadequate during evening and early morning hours when 95% of women feel unsafe. In Bhopal, Gwalior, and Jodhpur, none of the women who experienced harassment reported it to police — due to lack of trust, fear of shame, and normalization of daily harassment. Worse, transport providers who try to help women face penalties from contractors (lost workdays, no compensation), creating a system where bystander intervention is actively discouraged.

Trip Chaining & Time Poverty

Women make shorter, more frequent trips at varied times — combining work, childcare, and errands. In Bangalore, women traveled 1.7km for work vs 3.3km for men. Lower-income women use slower, cheaper modes, exacerbating time poverty and limiting employment opportunities.

Infrastructure Designed for Men

Women rank pavements, street lights, and parks as more important to their mobility than men do. Poorly lit areas, narrow pavements, and long distances to stops create barriers. Lower-income women are most dependent on public infrastructure — yet it is planned for a "neutral male user."

Mobility of Care Ignored

75% of unpaid care work is carried out by women, yet transport systems are designed for male commuting patterns. Women make shorter, more frequent trips combining work, childcare, and errands — but transport planning focuses on peak-hour individual trips. Trip-chaining increases travel time and distance, creating time poverty that limits economic participation.

Active Mobility Barriers

Women face disproportionate barriers to cycling and walking: fear of harassment, unsafe infrastructure, behavior of other road users, and crash testing based on average male physique. In Bogotá, women make up only 21% of cyclists. In the UK, women, elderly, disabled, and ethnic minorities are deterred from cycling by safety concerns. Yet active mobility is essential for first/last-mile connectivity.

The Four Stages of Women's Mobility

Every Journey Has Four Critical Points

Unlike male commuting patterns, women's travel involves complex trip-chaining (work + childcare + errands). Each stage presents unique barriers that can block access to opportunities entirely.

1

Access

Walking, cycling, or auto-rickshaw to reach primary transit stations through poorly lit, isolated pathways

2

Waiting

Heightened safety challenges at stations with inadequate lighting, seating, and no real-time information

3

Traveling

Overcrowding, harassment, and lack of reserved seating on main public transport services

4

Egress

Last-mile connectivity from transit stations to final destinations — the most vulnerable segment

Our Solution

Eight Pillars of Safe Mobility

Thaai Thadam integrates digital tools, community participation, and low-cost urban improvements into one coordinated ecosystem — going beyond fare subsidies to address the full spectrum of women's mobility barriers. Every pillar below maps to one of the hackathon's four output categories — Digital Tools, Pilot Projects, Awareness, or Policy Change — and is enhanced with evidence-based solutions from the Asian Development Bank's Gender Equality in Transport Toolkit (2025).

Safe Route Guidance

AI-powered routing that prioritizes well-lit streets, avoids isolated areas, and shows real-time safety scores for every path. Women vote on routes they need most — crowdsourced demand drives where smart bus stops and verified drivers are deployed next.

 Digital Tool Well-lit Paths Safety Scoring Crowdsourced Routes

Smart Mobility Hubs

Low-cost physical upgrades: solar lighting (30-40 lux for non-shopping areas, 25 lux for shopping areas per IRC 103:2012), seating, shade, QR codes for real-time info, and emergency contact displays. But more than a bus stop — a shared community space for gathering, shopping, and everyday errands. Designed with clear sightlines and adequate lighting to eliminate dead spaces — validated by Chennai's NMT policy where 80% of women rate the city as safe. Drawing from Jane Jacobs' "eyes on the street" principle, hubs are located near active commercial frontages with mixed land use — creating constant public presence that deters crime. Public toilets, an often-overlooked but critical facility for women caregivers, are included at every hub. Bus stop design uses transparent panels (not opaque advertisements) to maintain clear sightlines. Hawker and vendor zones near stops create natural surveillance — research from Bhopal, Gwalior, and Jodhpur confirms women feel safer where vendors are present. Public toilets are staffed by female attendants and operational during all service hours. Hubs are sited away from areas with drug and alcohol sales, which 96% of women in tier-2 Indian cities cited as a top safety concern.

 Pilot Project Solar Lighting QR Codes Community Space

Verified Last-Mile Transport

Partner with auto-rickshaw unions and women driver initiatives (Rapido Pink, MOWO model) to verify drivers, display ratings, and enable safe shared rides. B2B institutional partnerships with colleges, hospitals, and markets guarantee ridership and predictable driver income. Studies show 80% of women feel safer with women drivers. All intermediate public transport partners (autos, e-rickshaws) are GPS-registered and linked to a central control room with emergency panic buttons — addressing the unregulated IPT environment documented in Bhopal, Gwalior, and Jodhpur where lack of formalization left women without recourse.

 Digital Tool Driver Verification Women Drivers B2B Partnerships

Emergency Assistance

One-tap SOS with live location sharing, quick dial to police/ambulance/women's helpline, and display of nearest safe locations. Addresses the 95% of women who identify public transport as unsafe during off-peak hours.

 Digital Tool One-Tap SOS Live Location

Fare Integration & SADDD Data

Integrated fares across bus, auto, and shared rides eliminate the trip-chaining cost penalty women face when combining multiple modes. Gender-tagged electronic ticketing (SADDD — Sex-, Age-, Disability-Disaggregated Data) captures boarding patterns, travel times, and origin-destination data to inform service planning. CRUT Odisha's gender tagging revealed route-wise ridership patterns that enabled women-only buses and reserved seating decisions.

 Policy Change Fare Integration SADDD Data

Request-a-Stop & Women-Only Boarding

Women, girls, and older people can request drop-offs between designated bus stops after dark — reducing last-mile walking distances to their destination. Proven in Istanbul (5,000+ buses), Canada, and France. Women-only doors with female in-bus hosts on high-demand routes, plus priority seating for pregnant women, caregivers, and people with disabilities. Caution from Indian cities: In Bhopal, Gwalior, and Jodhpur, women-only buses failed because of limited route coverage, low frequency, and the inability of women traveling with male family members to use them. Request-a-stop avoids these pitfalls — it works across all existing routes without segregation.

 Policy Change Request-a-Stop Night Safety

Gender-Responsive Maintenance & Active Mobility

Drawing from the Karlskoga, Sweden precedent: maintenance schedules prioritize walkways, bike lanes, and public transit routes before car roads — because women walk, cycle, and use public transport more than men. Cycling infrastructure is designed with separation from motorized traffic, adequate lighting, and clear sightlines. Traffic calming measures (speed bumps, raised crosswalks, medians) slow motorized traffic near transit stops where women with children and goods cross. Intermediate Means of Transport (IMTs) such as cargo bicycles are promoted for women carrying heavy loads.

 Policy Change Gender-Responsive Maintenance Cycling Safety

Intersectional Approach & Gender Mainstreaming

Thaai Thadam moves beyond binary gender to an intersectional approach that recognizes overlapping discrimination: gender + class + disability + age + caste. Our Gender Advisory Committee includes women with disabilities, trans* persons, and marginalized caste representatives (50%+ women). Beyond employment as drivers, Thaai Thadam advocates for women in traffic enforcement, station management, planning, and operations — following evidence from Peru and Buenos Aires where women traffic officers reduced corruption.

How Thaai Thadam Works

Human-Centered Design

Seven Principles of Safe Mobility Hubs

Drawing from research on mobility hubs for women and caregivers in Chicago and Gothenburg — when we center the needs of women, transportation works better for everyone.

Trust & Security

A trusted place that feels like a trusted place. Clear sightlines, lighting, and visible safety features.

Convenience

Reliable, simple to use, and makes travel easier. Real-time info and seamless transfers between modes.

Comfort

Pleasant to wait, easy to transfer. Seating, shade, and protection from sun and rain.

Access & Ease

Easy to move around for people with physical challenges, strollers, or heavy bags.

Care & Belonging

A place where caregivers feel welcome. Space for children, elderly, and those who care for them.

Health & Safety

Clean and healthy for children, elderly, and caregivers. Emergency support always available.

Services & Affordability

Different and flexible travel options, plus access to food, shopping, and everyday errands. A hub is not just a transport node — it is a shared community space where people gather, shop, eat, and connect.

Research insight: When we center the needs of women and caregivers in mobility hub design, we make transportation systems work better for everyone — including the most vulnerable people in our cities. "A nice urban place is like a good party: guests stay because they are enjoying themselves." — Jan Gehl, Cities for People (2010)

Evidence Base

Research That Shapes Our Solution

Thaai Thadam is grounded in rigorous field research and proven interventions from across Indian cities. Our approach addresses systemic barriers, not just symptoms.

The "Pink Tax" on Mobility

Women spend 48% of travel costs on just 18% of distance — the first and last mile segments. Despite free bus schemes, women continue paying premium prices for perceived safer options. First and last-mile costs can completely offset savings from subsidized public transport.

— Beyond Free Rides: Multi-State Assessment, Nikore Associates & Sustainable Mobility Network, 2025

Safety at Transition Points

79% of women report harassment during transition points between transport modes. Safety infrastructure inside stations does not extend to surrounding areas where passengers wait for autos or walk to destinations. Security coverage stops at station boundaries.

— World Bank Toolkit for Gender Responsive Urban Mobility, 2022

Women Drivers = Safer Rides

Initiatives like Rapido Pink (2 lakh women captains), MOWO (2,500+ trained women drivers), and Azad Foundation (5,000+ women in transport) demonstrate that women-driven services create both employment and passenger safety. 80% of women report feeling safer with women drivers.

— Azad Foundation Annual Report 2024-25; MOWO Impact Data

Infrastructure Precedents

Chennai's NMT Policy (2014) dedicated 60% of transport budget to non-motorized infrastructure, resulting in 80% of women rating the city as safe. Bhubaneswar's Janpath Street redesign allocated adequate space for pedestrians and vendors, creating safer walking environments.

— Corporation of Chennai; Bhubaneswar Development Authority

My Safe Bus Pilot

Uttar Pradesh's Nirbhaya-funded pilot deployed 50 pink buses with GPS, CCTV, and panic buttons. Result: 1.5 lakh women benefited, 60% rise in perceived safety, 40% increase in timely harassment reporting, and 100+ women employed as drivers and conductors.

— UPSRTC Nirbhaya Fund Implementation, 2017

Trip Chaining Reality

Women's travel combines work, childcare, and household errands — requiring flexible schedules and frequent stoppages. 84% of women's work trips depend on public transport, yet systems are designed for male peak-hour, single-destination commuting patterns.

— World Bank; SPRF Women's Mobility Study, 2024

Crowdsourced Route Success

BUSSI, a Mexico-based van-sharing platform, proved that crowdsourced route voting works: users vote on needed routes, heat maps identify demand clusters, and the most-voted routes get officially opened. Result: 450+ active users in 3 months. B2B event transport (150+ people) provided guaranteed ridership and driver income.

— BUSSI Platform, Mexico City, 2015

Mobility Hubs for Women & Caregivers

Research from Chicago and Gothenburg with 66 women and caregivers reveals that mobility hubs must be more than transport nodes — they should be shared community spaces for gathering, shopping, eating, and everyday errands. Seven principles guide design: Trust, Convenience, Comfort, Access, Care, Health, and Services.

— Thynell, Wexsus / University of Gothenburg / Shared-Use Mobility Center, 2024

Designated Waiting Areas (Toronto TTC)

Toronto Transport Commission pioneered DWAs — well-lit, intercom-equipped safe zones on subway platforms with direct communication to station operators. Their "Between Stops" program allows women to request stops between designated halts between 9pm-5am. Transparent bus shelters provide visibility. Montreal adopted similar programs. These proven interventions directly address the waiting-stage vulnerability.

— Toronto Transport Commission; ITDP & Safetipin Policy Brief, 2017

Women's Safety Audit (WSA) Methodology

The UN Women-recommended Women's Safety Audit evaluates public spaces through nine validated parameters: (1) Lighting levels and functionality; (2) Openness — clear sightlines and visibility; (3) Visibility — absence of overhanging trees, high walls, or obstructions; (4) Crowding/emptiness — balanced occupancy that deters crime without enabling harassment; (5) Security presence — guards, police, or active monitoring; (6) Walk/footpath condition — surface quality, width, accessibility for strollers and wheelchairs; (7) Public transport availability — proximity and frequency of services; (8) Usage patterns — mixed-gender presence vs. male-dominated spaces; (9) Emotional response — subjective feeling of safety and comfort. Conducted through participatory walks by groups of 3-6 women with planners and officials, the WSA legitimizes women's concerns and builds community safety ownership. Thaai Thadam's quarterly gender audits directly apply this framework.

— UN Women Safe Cities Programme; GIZ-SUTP Module 7a; Women in Cities International

Gender Sensitization Training at Scale

Jagori initiated gender sensitization for DTC drivers using role-plays where drivers sat in buses watching how women experienced the ride. Manas Foundation scaled this to 100,000+ transport personnel including auto, taxi, and bus drivers. The "Auto-Sahara" and "Taxi-Sahara" helplines create ongoing two-way dialogue. Drivers receive certification stickers after training — a replicable model for Trichy.

— Jagori 2012; Manas Foundation; ITDP & Safetipin Policy Brief, 2017

Seoul Women Friendly City Project

Seoul's comprehensive approach included lowering bus/subway handles, installing diaper-changing tables, improving restroom lighting, designating women parking zones, and launching the "Women Safety Brand Call Taxi" — sending plate numbers and locations via SMS to family members. A 187-member partner group with women's civic groups and experts guided implementation. Education programs trained government officials in gender mainstreaming.

— UN Habitat, Women Friendly City Project, Seoul; ITDP & Safetipin Policy Brief, 2017

EIGE Gender-Sensitive Infrastructure Survey

A survey of 5,378 women and men across 28 EU countries found women perceive all four urban mobility infrastructure types — public transport, pavements, street lights, and parks — as more important to everyday life than men. 42% of women said public transport was "very important" for employment vs 33% of men. Lower-income women were most dependent. The study concludes: "Gender mainstreaming in urban mobility can support sustainable economic growth and employment."

— European Institute for Gender Equality (EIGE), Gender-Sensitive Infrastructure Factsheet, 2020

Mexico City Bystander Intervention App

In Mexico City, 65% of women experience violence in transit. The World Bank partnered with transport ministries, bus companies, NGOs, and app developers to create a mobile application enabling passengers to report sexual harassment and trigger non-confrontational bystander interventions. The app compiles data for diagnostics and policy attention. This proves that tech-enabled community reporting can scale safety interventions in dense urban transit systems — directly informing Thaai Thadam's SOS and crowdsourced reporting features.

— World Bank Group; UNFCCC TEC Brief #18, 2024

Sri Lanka "Not On My Bus" Campaign

Co-created by Oxfam and local partners, this campaign reduced sexual harassment in public transport through bystander intervention. Targeting bus drivers, conductors, young people, and rush-hour commuters in English, Tamil, and Sinhala, it used social media campaigns and government dialogues to shift social norms. The key insight: behavioral change among transport workers and fellow passengers is as critical as infrastructure. This validates Thaai Thadam's dual approach of driver verification + community reporting.

— Oxfam; UNFCCC TEC Brief #18, 2024

Kochi Metro: 80% Women Workforce

Supported by AFD and the Indian Government, the Kochi metro stands out with nearly 80% of its team being women — working as train drivers, station managers, ticket machine operators, and cleaning staff. The project demonstrates that women-led transport operations are viable at scale in India. Combined with the Pune and Bangalore metro lines (33% women drivers and station controllers, with crèche facilities and shift flexibility), this provides a replicable model for Thaai Thadam's women driver partnerships.

— Agence Française de Développement (AFD); European Investment Bank (EIB); UNFCCC TEC Brief #18, 2024

ADB Gender Equality in Transport Toolkit (2025)

The ADB's comprehensive toolkit provides a gender-transformative framework for transport systems in Asia and the Pacific. Key recommendations directly integrated into Thaai Thadam: (1) SADDD data collection (Sex-, Age-, Disability-Disaggregated Data) through gender-tagged electronic ticketing; (2) Request-a-stop services after dark for women, girls, and older people; (3) Fare integration across modes to eliminate trip-chaining penalties; (4) Women-only doors and female in-bus hosts on high-demand routes; (5) Quarterly gender audits of infrastructure and services; (6) CCTV with ML/AI for harassment detection; (7) Women-operated first/last-mile services with priority parking at transit stops.

— Asian Development Bank, Gender Equality in Transport Tool Kit: Moving Toward Gender Transformative Transport Systems, August 2025

Istanbul Request-a-Stop: 5,000+ Buses

In 2016, Istanbul Metropolitan Municipality implemented a request-a-stop policy across more than 5,000 buses — allowing women to request drop-offs anywhere along the route after 10 p.m. This was executed by the Istanbul Electric Tram and Tunnel Company. The policy directly addresses the most vulnerable segment of women's journeys: the last-mile walk from bus stops to home in darkness. Proven, scalable, and low-cost — requiring only driver training and protocol changes, not infrastructure investment.

— European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD), Guide for Municipalities, 2022; ADB Gender Equality in Transport Tool Kit, 2025

CRUT Odisha: Gender-Tagged Electronic Ticketing

Capital Region Urban Transport (CRUT) in Odisha partnered with SMART-SUT to incorporate gender tagging in electronic ticketing machines, enabling collection of sex and age data across adults, senior citizens, and children. The system captures route names, boarding-alighting patterns, and travel demand — allowing the agency to make informed decisions on women-only buses, reserved seating, female conductors, and scheduling during peak hours. Data-driven gender responsiveness replaces assumptions with evidence.

— GIZ, 2024; ADB Gender Equality in Transport Tool Kit, 2025

Davao BRT: CCTV with AI & GBV Desks

The Davao Public Transport Modernization Project (Philippines, 2023-2030) introduces 350 electric buses with CCTV cameras to prevent harassment, supported by an intelligent transport system. Bus stops provide information on access to emergency response including violence against women desks and emergency hotlines. Terminals include lactation stations, gender-inclusive restrooms, and rest areas for pregnant women. A target of 10% women among security personnel and comprehensive SEAH/GBV training for all transport personnel.

— ADB, Davao Public Transport Modernization Project, 2023

Kyrgyz Republic: 20% Women Bus Drivers

The Urban Transport Electrification Project in Bishkek, Kyrgyz Republic targets 20% women as bus drivers and 40% women as technical staff for electric bus routes (from a baseline of 36%). Women are retained through safe and decent work conditions: nondiscrimination policies, fair promotion, employee support including access to psychologists, and gender-sensitive guidelines for career advancement. Demonstrates that women in technical transport roles are achievable with intentional policy.

— ADB, Urban Transport Electrification Project, Kyrgyz Republic, 2021

Nepal Road Maintenance: 70% Women Groups

The Strengthening the National Rural Transport Program in Nepal prioritized recruitment of poor women from marginalized communities for road maintenance groups (RMGs). Results: 70%+ of RMGs composed of women, 68% linked to cooperatives for income activities, 13% saved more than $490 enabling small business investments, and exposure to leadership skills encouraged participation in local elections — with 4 RMG members elected as ward-level leaders. Proves transport projects can be vehicles for women's economic and political empowerment.

— World Bank, Strengthening the National Rural Transport Program, Nepal, 2013-2019

Karlskoga, Sweden: Gender-Responsive Maintenance

The municipality of Karlskoga discovered that clearing snow from main roads first before walkways and bike lanes created a gender bias — since women walk, cycle, and use public transport more than men. After collecting gender-disaggregated data, they reversed the schedule: walkways and public transit routes cleared first. Results: traffic injuries halved, costs reduced (injuries cost more than road maintenance), and the process was replicated in Stockholm with similar success. This proves that maintenance scheduling is a gender equity issue, not just an operational one.

— Karlskoga Municipality, Sweden; MobiliseYourCity / GIZ TUMI, Introduction to Gender and Transport, 2024

Gender-Responsive vs. Gender-Sensitive vs. Gender-Transformative

The MobiliseYourCity framework distinguishes five levels of gender engagement: Gender-Negative (upholds inequality), Gender-Blind (ignores it), Gender-Sensitive (recognizes but does not act), Gender-Responsive (actively combats inequality), and Gender-Transformative (addresses deep roots of unequal norms). Thaai Thadam aims for gender-transformative impact by: shifting maintenance priorities, creating women-led employment in transport, collecting intersectional data, and ensuring 50%+ women in decision-making bodies. Moving beyond sensitivity to action-oriented transformation.

— MobiliseYourCity / GIZ TUMI; NDC Partnership; EbA (Ecosystem-based Adaptation), 2024

The Gender Data Gap in Transport

Transport planning suffers from a severe gender data gap. Most cities lack sex-disaggregated data on travel patterns, mode choice, safety incidents, or maintenance needs. The MobiliseYourCity training emphasizes collecting both quantitative data (travel time, income, distance) and qualitative data (use of time, reasons for mobility choices, safety perceptions). Timing matters: peak travel times differ by gender. Women travel more during working hours for care-related trips, while men dominate traditional rush hours. Without this data, planning perpetuates male-biased infrastructure.

— MobiliseYourCity / GIZ TUMI; Despacio (2020); EIGE

Active Mobility: A Gendered Safety Issue

Women face disproportionate barriers to cycling and walking: fear of harassment, unsafe infrastructure, behavior of other road users, and crash testing based on average male physique. In Bogotá, women are only 21% of cyclists; in the UK, women, elderly, disabled, and ethnic minorities are deterred from cycling. The UN Second Decade of Action for Road Safety (2021-2030) calls for integration of gender in all road safety policymaking. Women are more likely affected as pedestrians and car passengers in crashes, while men are more likely affected as drivers. Safe active mobility infrastructure is essential for women's first/last-mile connectivity.

— MobiliseYourCity / GIZ TUMI; UN Second Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021-2030; Despacio (2020)

Mobility of Care: The Hidden Transport Burden

75% of unpaid care work is carried out by women, generating complex trip-chaining patterns that transport planning ignores. Women make shorter, more frequent trips at varied times — combining work, childcare, eldercare, and household errands. In Bogotá, women spend 11% more time commuting than men, with larger differences in low-income neighborhoods. Conventional transport planning focuses on individual peak-hour commuting, marginalizing care-related mobility. SDG Target 5.4 explicitly calls for recognizing and valuing unpaid care work through public services and infrastructure.

— MobiliseYourCity / GIZ TUMI; UN Women; SDG 5.4

Beyond the Binary: Gender Diversity in Transport

Gender-responsive policies must go beyond catering to two gender identities. 350 trans* and gender-diverse people were murdered in 2020, with 82% in Central and South America. In the US, 22% of trans* people reported harassment on buses, trains, and taxis; 4% reported physical assault; 9% reported denial of equal treatment. Currently, almost no data exists on mobility discrimination of non-binary people in the Global South. Thaai Thadam commits to inclusive data collection and infrastructure design that serves all gender identities, including gender-neutral facilities and trans*-inclusive safety protocols.

— MobiliseYourCity / GIZ TUMI; National Transgender Discrimination Survey (NTDS), USA; Dazé and Terton (2021)

Safetipin Safety Audits: Bhopal, Gwalior & Jodhpur

A comprehensive study by The Asia Foundation, Safetipin, and KOICA collected 9,133 safety audit pins across three tier-2 Indian cities using the Safetipin app's nine-parameter audit (lighting, openness, visibility, crowd, security, walkpath, public transport availability, gender usage, and feeling). Key findings: average safety scores ranged from 2.1–2.9/5, indicating significant scope for improvement. The Public Transport parameter was rated below 2/3 in all three cities — below average in Bhopal and poor in Gwalior and Jodhpur. Lighting, walkpath, and gender usage were the parameters most strongly correlated with women's feeling of safety. Critically, 29% of women reported experiencing sexual harassment in the past year, but this is widely understood to be underreported due to normalization of daily harassment, fear of family shame, and zero trust in police response. The study validates that tier-2 Indian cities face identical mobility barriers to Trichy and that data-driven, participatory safety audits are essential for targeted intervention.

— The Asia Foundation, Safetipin, KOICA: "Women and Mobility: A Case Study of Bhopal, Gwalior & Jodhpur"

Phased Rollout Plan

Starting small, learning fast, and scaling responsibly across Trichy — backed by proven precedents from across India.

1
Months 1-3

Pilot Phase

Launch at 10-15 high-traffic bus stops in one ward. Install QR codes, improve lighting and signage with clear sightlines, onboard 50 verified drivers (including women drivers via MOWO/Rapido Pink model), and deploy the app prototype. B2B partnerships with Bharathidasan University and Govt Hospital guarantee initial ridership. Implement gender-tagged electronic ticketing (SADDD) to collect sex-, age-, and disability-disaggregated data. Pilot request-a-stop on evening routes after 7pm. Conduct first quarterly gender audit with an intersectional lens — including women with disabilities, trans* persons, and marginalized caste representatives. Collect both quantitative data (travel time, mode, distance) and qualitative data (safety perceptions, care-related trip purposes, time poverty). The pilot is structured as a Gender Action Plan (GAP) with 10-year vision, annual monitoring, and 5-year review cycles, following the GIZ-SUTP framework: Who does what? How? When? With whom? Who has what? Who decides? Who benefits? Who loses? Community consultations provide childcare facilities so women caregivers can participate in planning meetings — removing a practical barrier that otherwise excludes their voices.
